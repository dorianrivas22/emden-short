## Facts (checked 13/11/2026)
- 1850s London: sewage and cesspits drained into the Thames, which was also a source of drinking water.
  Cholera epidemics killed tens of thousands; most doctors believed in miasma (disease from bad air / smell).
  Sources: Wikipedia "Great Stink"; Science Museum "Cholera in Victorian London".
- Summer 1858, the "Great Stink": hot weather, the river reeked; Parliament's curtains were soaked in chloride of
  lime; MPs held handkerchiefs to their noses; Disraeli called the Thames "a Stygian pool". The bill was proposed
  on 15 July and passed in a record 18 days. Sources: London Museum "The Great Stink of 1858"; Mental Floss;
  Museum of Oxford.
- Joseph Bazalgette, chief engineer of the Metropolitan Board of Works, sized each pipe for the densest population
  and most generous sewage allowance, then doubled the diameter "for the unforeseen". (The often-quoted line
  "we're only going to do this once" has no clear primary source: do not quote it.) Source: Institution of Civil
  Engineers "London sewer system"; London on the Ground.
- Intercepting sewers built 1859–1865, wider system to ~1875; 318 million bricks; ~82 miles of main sewers,
  ~1,100 miles of street sewers. Sources: Wikipedia "London sewer system", "Joseph Bazalgette"; Thames Water.
- The Victoria Embankment (1865–1870) was built on reclaimed river land over the low-level sewer, with the
  District line railway inside it. Source: Wikipedia "Thames Embankment".
- The system took sewage away from the drinking water and so ended cholera in the areas it served, although it was
  built to remove the smell. The last London epidemic (1866) hit East London, which was not yet connected; Farr
  traced it to the Old Ford reservoir water. Sources: Wikipedia "London sewer system"; Geological Society
  "The blue plague"; Science Museum.
- The system still carries London's sewage; London now has well over twice the population it was designed for;
  a new "super sewer" (Thames Tideway Tunnel) was only completed in 2025. Source: Wikipedia "Thames Tideway Tunnel".

## Script (English)
Everyone laughed when he made London's sewers twice as big as they needed to be.
You see, back then, every toilet in London emptied straight into the river. The same river people drank from.
And people kept dying of cholera, but the doctors were sure it came from the smell.
So when he asked for pipes twice as wide, the politicians thought he was mad. Twice the bricks? For a smell?
Then came the summer of 1858. The river cooked like soup, and the stink rolled right into Parliament.
They soaked the curtains in bleach. They held hankies to their noses. They called it the Great Stink.
And in just eighteen days, they gave him everything.
Three hundred million bricks. Tunnels you could drive a carriage through. And on top, a brand new riverside road, with a train line hidden inside.
That road is the Embankment, and trains still rumble through it today.
But he was wrong about one thing.
Cholera never came from the smell. It came from the water. And by carrying the filth away from the river, his tunnels stopped it anyway.
When it struck one last time, it hit the only part of town his sewers hadn't reached yet.
Today, London has more than twice the people he planned for, and his tunnels still work. Because...

## Guion (español)
Todos se rieron cuando hizo las alcantarillas de Londres el doble de grandes de lo necesario.
Verás, entonces, cada váter de Londres desaguaba directamente en el río. El mismo río del que bebía la gente.
Y la gente seguía muriendo de cólera, pero los médicos estaban seguros de que venía del olor.
Así que cuando pidió tuberías el doble de anchas, los políticos pensaron que estaba loco. ¿El doble de ladrillos? ¿Por un olor?
Entonces llegó el verano de mil ochocientos cincuenta y ocho. El río se coció como una sopa, y la peste entró de lleno en el Parlamento.
Empaparon las cortinas en lejía. Se taparon la nariz con pañuelos. Lo llamaron la Gran Peste.
Y en solo dieciocho días, le dieron todo.
Trescientos millones de ladrillos. Túneles por los que cabía un carruaje. Y encima, un paseo nuevo junto al río, con un tren escondido dentro.
Ese paseo es el Embankment, y los trenes todavía pasan por dentro.
Pero se equivocaba en una cosa.
El cólera nunca vino del olor. Venía del agua. Y al llevarse la porquería lejos del río, sus túneles lo frenaron igualmente.
Cuando atacó por última vez, golpeó la única parte de la ciudad a la que sus alcantarillas aún no habían llegado.
Hoy, Londres tiene más del doble de gente de la que él calculó, y sus túneles siguen funcionando. Porque...
